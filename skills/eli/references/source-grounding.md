# Source grounding by subject

Use the relevant section when the explanation depends on supplied documents, numbers, or an uncertain situation. This guide preserves the broad subject scope of ELI without forcing every subject into a causal mechanism. It is not a substitute for reading the source.

## Keep evidence separate from illustration

Use the source's definitions, organization, and meaningful distinctions as the basis. Simplify wording, not the actual rule. Distinguish documented facts from your interpretation, transparent calculations, and outside context. When the task is source-only, do not silently replace the source with outside material. Flag any apparent discrepancy; verify separately only when requested or required by the host.

Choose the smallest sufficient source set. Cite a clause, section, page, row, or source passage that actually supports the claim, using the host's citation system where available. In a standalone output, use readable source names and locators; include usable links only when available and appropriate for the recipient. Do not copy host-only citation tokens into HTML and assume they become usable links.

Keep fictional teaching examples labeled and separate. A fictional threshold in a worked example is not a default for the user's product. Omission is not evidence of absence: "The supplied document does not state a cancellation right" is different from "There is no cancellation right."

## Code and errors

For code, identify purpose, inputs, relevant state, operations, and outputs before syntax. Read the implementation and context needed for the question rather than inferring behavior from a filename or comment. Distinguish what the code can do from what a supplied execution trace proves it did.

For an error, separate the observed failure from candidate causes. An error message can establish a symptom without establishing a root cause. Explain the next discriminating check when useful, but do not turn an explanation request into unsolicited configuration changes. Trace actual code or reproduce behavior only within the available tools and permissions.

## Documents, policies, and contracts

Identify scope, affected parties, obligations or permissions, conditions, deadlines, consequences, and exceptions. Preserve consequential words such as "may", "must", "unless", "over", and "within". Flag interacting clauses and undefined terms.

Give a routine scenario and one edge case only when the text supports them. Separate plain-language explanation from professional advice or a definitive legal interpretation. Do not turn a short excerpt into a claim about an entire agreement.

For a **new policy**, explain the documented rules. Say what changed only when a prior version or explicit change summary establishes the old state. If that baseline is absent, state the limit and continue explaining the available rules. Missing comparison data does not prevent a useful explanation of the present document.

## Deals, pricing, and commercial terms

Make clear who gets what, what each party commits to, units, billing periods, usage conditions, and any specified tiers, renewals, or overages. Do not assume a quote's validity date is its contract end date, or that an included allowance rolls over when unused.

Use documented figures for real calculations. Show units and method, and label the answer as calculated. Where a teaching scenario needs a hypothetical quantity, state that it is an illustration rather than a usage forecast. Preserve unknown taxes, fees, renewal conditions, and discounts as unknown.

For a **renewal**, a proposal can establish proposed terms without establishing current terms. Compare only when both exist. Do not invent relationship effects, revenue recognition, customer intentions, or usage forecasts. Supporting arithmetic belongs in an explanation; a full pricing strategy or proposal is additional work unless requested.

## Data, metrics, and dashboards

Establish the metric definition, numerator and denominator when relevant, units, population, date range, and coverage. Check whether the figures use comparable definitions and populations before computing a difference. Distinguish zero, missing, not applicable, and not yet reported.

A change in an aggregate does not establish its driver. Correlation does not identify a cause. A partial-period result is not a completed-period total. Explain what the measure helps answer and what it leaves unresolved, especially when the source supplies an interpretation stronger than the data alone supports.

Show the calculation without overselling its implications. A percentage-point difference is not the same as a relative percentage change. Keep averages tied to the populations used to calculate them.

## Processes and workflows

Follow the actual sequence, hand-offs, required inputs, branches, and outputs. Explain a step's rationale when documented or clearly established by the mechanism. Otherwise flag it as unknown instead of inventing why an organization chose it.

A documented procedure and a report of actual practice are different sources. Identify disagreement rather than silently reconciling them. Do not suggest you verified a live workflow when you only read its description.

## Decisions, strategy, and organizational changes

Separate what was decided, its effective date, affected groups, stated rationale, documented alternatives, and unresolved details. Attribute statements of rationale to their source. Explain relevant consequences conditionally when implementation details are incomplete.

Do not infer motives, certainty, endorsement, or a missing "before" state from a polished announcement. Audience adaptation should clarify facts and implications, not steer the recipient by inventing certainty or emphasizing only one side of a contested interpretation.

## Events and uncertain situations

Establish what happened and when, what is verified, and which accounts are attributed or disputed. Distinguish event date from publication date. Verify changeable facts when permitted and required; otherwise state the knowledge or source limitation.

Do not transform a timeline into a proven chain of causes. Explain supported contributing factors and established consequences, with uncertainty where warranted. When the source does not answer why, an honest account of what is known is the explanation.

## Ideas and concepts

Identify the underlying relationships, structure, meaning, or mechanism appropriate to the subject. Introduce essential terms and connect examples back to them. A model can simplify the details without changing the relationships it claims to explain.

Mark the boundary between an illustrative model and empirical claims. Do not use invented numbers as scientific measurements or an anthropomorphic story as an account of a system's literal mental states. Preserve the conditions under which the explanation holds.

## Sharing and combined requests

Determine the intended recipient from the task. Include private names or exact figures only when necessary and appropriate. For public materials, use clearly marked generalized or fictional examples rather than silently redacting or changing a source's meaning. Do not embed credentials, sensitive internal links, or raw customer records in a shareable page by default.

For a combined request, carry the same evidence discipline into the second deliverable. Explaining a policy and drafting a message does not authorize sending the message. Explaining a workflow and creating a page does not authorize publishing internal material.
