# Explanation patterns

Consult this guide when a substantial explanation needs a teaching approach or an earlier explanation did not land. Select one main pattern, not a collection of obligatory sections. These are ELI authoring choices, not a claim that one method fits every learner.

## Choose the shape of understanding

| The reader needs to understand... | Start with... | What makes the pattern useful |
|---|---|---|
| A process or system | Follow one item | Make each hand-off and change visible. |
| Two easily confused ideas | Contrast a matched pair | Hold the situation constant and isolate the relevant difference. |
| A rule, limit, or tier | Show a boundary | Compare cases just below, at, and above the threshold. |
| A number or metric | Build it from parts | Show the denominator, unit, time window, and a changed input. |
| A clause, policy, or commitment | Condition to consequence | Identify who does what, under which conditions, with what exceptions. |
| An unfamiliar concept with a useful familiar parallel | Analogy with a mapping | Move from the familiar situation to the real terms, then test the limit. |
| A decision or uncertain event | Separate what is established | Distinguish documented facts, attributed explanations, implications, and unknowns. |

## Follow one item

Choose a representative object: one request, package, application, message, or payment. Establish its starting state and follow it through the actual steps. At each important step, say what changes, what enables the next step, and who or what is responsible.

Use this instead of naming every component and hoping the reader assembles the process. Branch only where a branch changes the idea being taught. A documented workflow is not proof that every real case follows it; preserve that distinction.

**Pitfall:** a row of boxes labeled "Input -> Processing -> Output" names stages but does not explain the transformation.

## Contrast a matched pair

Use the same context to show two concepts side by side. Explain the decisive difference, what each does, and where a common assumption fails. A worked case is often clearer than two dictionary definitions.

**Fictional example:** one service rule caps simultaneous active jobs; a different rule caps total starts within a fixed minute. Compare them under the same sequence of job starts and completions. State the demo rules rather than assuming a particular real product behaves that way.

**Pitfall:** changing both the context and the rule makes the comparison hard to interpret. Do not force a binary when the concepts overlap.

## Show a boundary

Write the exact condition before choosing numbers. Test below, at, and above the boundary using the same units. Explain what changes at the boundary and what stays the same. This works for permissions, eligibility, caps, thresholds, and pricing rules.

If the source uses "over", do not silently interpret it as "at least". Do not turn a progressive tier into a price that applies retroactively to all units, or the reverse.

**Pitfall:** a smooth slider animation can hide a rule with a discontinuity. The rule, not the animation, determines the outcome.

## Build a number from parts

Name what is counted, the population, the unit, and the period. Show a small calculation, then change one input while holding the others fixed. Explain what the result can establish and what remains unknown.

**Fictional example:** 18 of 20 items pass a check, so the pass rate is 90%. Increasing the numerator from 18 to 19 while keeping the total at 20 raises the rate to 95%. A larger population with different composition is not the same controlled comparison.

**Pitfall:** an aggregate can change because the mix of cases changed. Do not imply that a metric movement by itself proves an intervention worked.

## Condition to consequence

Translate a rule into: **who**, **trigger**, **action or permission**, **consequence**, and **exception**. Include only the parts the source establishes. Walk through a routine case and an important edge case. Tie consequential statements to the actual clause or section.

This is often better than a metaphor for contracts and policies. When two clauses interact, show the interaction rather than treating one clause as the whole document.

**Pitfall:** "This usually means..." can smuggle a standard industry term into a document that never states it.

## Analogy with a mapping

Choose a familiar situation that preserves the relationship being taught, not just a visual resemblance. Establish the minimum useful mapping: "this part corresponds to that part; this action corresponds to that action." Return to the real subject's vocabulary before finishing.

State a limit when it blocks a likely false inference. A short limitation is enough; do not bury the main idea under every difference. Then use a different example of the real subject so understanding does not depend entirely on the metaphor.

A learner's own analogy can be useful evidence: check the relationships and consequences, not whether their wording matches yours. Avoid assuming familiarity with parenting, homeownership, sports, or a profession from age or relationship labels alone.

**Pitfall:** a charming analogy can explain the wrong thing perfectly. Remove it if it distorts the mechanism or meaning.

## Separate what is established

For ambiguous situations, organize by evidence rather than inventing a causal story:

- What is directly established, including the relevant date and source.
- What identified people or documents say it means and the limits of that evidence.
- What follows under stated conditions, and what is still unknown.

Do not fill an unknown motive with a plausible explanation. Documented options and trade-offs are different from alternatives you introduce for illustration. Make that distinction visible.

**Pitfall:** sequence is not causation. Two events occurring together or in order does not establish why they happened.

## Adapt the pattern to the goal

For **Like I'm five**, select the smallest accurate case and use concrete language. For **Make it click**, connect the example to the underlying idea and include a changed case when length permits. For **Help me explain it**, give the recipient-facing version first and separate preparation notes or likely questions. For **Help me use it**, tie each necessary action to the reason it works. For **Give me the big picture**, retain only the relationships needed to orient the reader.

Choose depth from the requested outcome, not a fixed word count. Respect explicit brevity even when a pattern offers more possibilities.

## Optional checks and targeted repair

A check should reveal a specific understanding gap. Ask the learner to predict a changed outcome, distinguish two cases, or restate the idea using their own situation. Make the answer or feedback available without a reply unless they requested a conversational lesson.

When the learner struggles, identify whether the gap is a term, a relationship, a condition, or a misleading analogy. Repair that gap with a smaller example, then return to the original idea. Do not respond by merely adding more jargon or repeating a longer version of the same metaphor.
